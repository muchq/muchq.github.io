A Goldbach-type problem for the Liouville function

Spreading the Blame

A counting trick that turns an exponential bound into |Eq| ≤ q³·|E|, explained with the clock, the lattice and the receipts it runs on.

Andy · 7 November 2026 · not peer reviewed

Written with substantial help from Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic: the proofs, the Lean code, the figures and this text were all produced with it. Andy is responsible for the content. Errors are ours; please report them.

The Liouville function λ(n) = (−1)Ω(n) is −1 when n has an odd number of prime factors (counted with multiplicity) and +1 otherwise. It is completely multiplicative: λ(ab) = λ(a)λ(b). A Goldbach-type question asks how it behaves on the two parts of a sum. Fix a prime p and look at

L(p) = Σ0<n<p λ(n)·λ(p − n).Each term is ±1, so |L(p)| ≤ p − 1. The question is how close to that edge L(p) can get.

If L(p) were exactly ±(p − 1), every product λ(n)λ(p − n) would have the same sign. The aim is to show that many of them don't. The rest of this page builds the one tool that does the work: a bound on how often a single "wrong" pair can be blamed.

Where this sits. The qualitative question, whether every even N > 2 is a + b with λ(a) = λ(b) = −1, was asked on MathOverflow in 2018 [5] and is attributed to Shusterman in [2]. Mangerel proved |L(N)| < N − 1 for all large N [1], answering a question from the 2018 AIM workshop on Sarnak's conjecture. Assuming GRH, he showed that every sign pattern of (λ(n), λ(N − n)) occurs ≫ N·e−C(log log N)6 times for large primes N, which settles the question for large even N under GRH [2]. In September 2026 a public repository posted an unconditional proof of the qualitative statement, with a Lean formalization [6]; we have not checked it. This page is about one quantitative step in the approach of [2], not the qualitative question.

Colouring a clock

Put 1, 2, …, p − 1 around a clock with 0 ≡ p at the top. Then n and p − n are mirror images, joined by a horizontal chord. Write ε(n) = λ(n)λ(p − n) for the sign carried by that chord, and let ε₀ be the majority sign.Lean: liouville

Multiplying wraps around

Multiplication by q shuffles the clock: x ↦ qx mod p. As long as qx < p nothing wraps, and multiplicativity gives λ(qx) = λ(q)λ(x) for free. After the hand passes 12 o'clock, qx mod p is a different integer and the rule can fail. Call x a defect of q when

λ(qx mod p) · λ(q) · λ(x) = −1,and write Eq for the set of defects.

Crossing the wrap costs one mirror pair

There is exactly one honest way to relate numbers on opposite sides of the wrap. If four positive integers satisfy a·b + c·d = p, then ab = p − cd, so

λ(a)λ(b) = λ(p − cd) = ε(cd) · λ(c)λ(d).One application costs one mirror pair, the chord through cd.

A certificate for the point x is a list of chords whose signs multiply to λ(qx mod p)λ(q)λ(x). It has to work for every completely multiplicative ±1 function at once, so it can only be built from identities like the one above. If every chord in the list is green, the product is ε₀ raised to an even power, which is +1, and x cannot be a defect. So every defect can blame a red chord in its own certificate.Lean: refl_step Lean: Valid

|Eq| ≤ Σred a load(a) ≤ (max load) · |E|load(a) = how many certificates use the point a

Everything hinges on the max load. If one red chord could be blamed by thousands of defects, a handful of red chords could explain everything. The old bounds allowed exactly that: 4q in an earlier version of this project, 2q² in [2, Prop. 4.1]. The new construction keeps it at q³.Lean: load

Walking down a lattice

Fix a point z and look at all integer pairs (Q, R) with Qz ≡ R (mod p). They form a lattice. Pairs with R > 0 say "λ(R) = λ(Q)λ(z)", pairs with R < 0 say the same about p − |R|. Two pairs on opposite sides of the axis whose parallelogram has area exactly p are related by one reflection identity: the two rectangles Qu·|Rw| and Qw·Ru add up to p.

Below the hyperbola Q·|R| = p, every primitive pair has such a partner with smaller Q. So from any target under the hyperbola you can walk down, one parallelogram at a time, to Q = 1, where the statement is trivially true. Each step drops one certificate point a = Qw·Ru.Lean: descent

Two ways around a square

Write Δ(m, x) = λ(mx mod p)λ(m)λ(x), so x is a defect of m exactly when Δ(m, x) = −1. Multiplying by m and then by d lands on the same point as multiplying by d and then by m. Following the defect both ways gives

Δ(m, x) · Δ(d, x) = Δ(d, mx) · Δ(m, dx).Both sides compute the defect of md at x.

The square itself is not new. It is a form of the reciprocity identity in [2, §2.2.2, Lemma 4.3], and [6] formalizes a commuting square for the multipliers −2 and −3. The change here is to apply it with the point x held fixed.

By the pigeonhole principle (Dirichlet's approximation argument) there is some d < m that sends mx within p/m of 0 or of p. Call the landing residue ±r. Then both factors on the right are lattice targets under the hyperbola, one in the lattice of mx and one in the lattice of dx, and both end at the same residue, so their bare reflections cancel. What is left is Δ(d, x): the same point with a smaller multiplier. Repeat until the multiplier is 1.Lean: dirichlet Lean: square_mod Lean: poly_cert

Every use comes with a receipt

Now the count. A point a in the certificate of x was produced in the lattice of z = mx mod p for some multiplier m ≤ q, at a step with denominators Qu, Qw ≤ q, as a = Qw·Ru with Qu·z ≡ Ru. Keep the triple (m, Qu, Qw) as a receipt. From the receipt and a alone you can rebuild x:

x ≡ (a / Qw) · (Qu · m)−1 (mod p).Two different points x can never share a receipt.

There are only q · q · q possible receipts. So no point is used by more than q³ certificates, whatever p is. That is the whole proof of the load bound, and it is short enough that a computer checked every line of it.Lean: Anchored Lean: poly_load

What the bound buys

Put the pieces together: |Eq| ≤ q³·|E| for every prime p and every q < p, for either choice of sign. An analytic input that forces many defects for some small q now forces many red chords.

Under the generalized Riemann hypothesis, [2, Prop. 2.2] gives a prime q of size about (log p)² with |Eq| ≫ p. With the cubic bound this gives |L(p)| ≤ p − c·p/(log p)6+ε; the bound in [2] is p − p·e−C(log log p)6. Without GRH, Burgess's character-sum bound [3] at scale p1/4+ε plays the same role and gives a power saving p1/2−ε for one of the two signs.

The load bound, the descent and the square are formalized in Lean 4 [8] with Mathlib [9] and use only the standard axioms. The analytic inputs (Burgess's bound, and Prop. 2.2 of [2]) enter as stated hypotheses and are not formalized. Whether these results are new still needs an expert check against recent literature.
